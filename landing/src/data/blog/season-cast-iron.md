# How to Season a Cast Iron Pan (And Why Everyone Overcomplicates It)

The internet has turned cast iron seasoning into a ritual involving specific oil ratios, precise oven temperatures, and hours of waiting. Here's the truth: it's simpler than you think, and your great-grandmother didn't need a YouTube tutorial.

## What Seasoning Actually Is

Seasoning isn't a coating you apply — it's **polymerized oil**. When oil is heated past its smoke point, its molecules bond together and to the iron surface, creating a hard, non-stick layer. That's it. No magic.

## The Simple Method

### What You Need
- Your cast iron pan
- Any high-smoke-point cooking oil (vegetable, canola, grapeseed, or flaxseed)
- Paper towels
- An oven

### Steps

1. **Clean the pan.** If it's new, wash with warm soapy water (yes, soap is fine — more on that later). If it's old and rusty, scrub with steel wool until you're down to bare metal.

2. **Dry completely.** Put it on the stove over low heat for 2 minutes to evaporate all moisture. Moisture is iron's enemy.

3. **Apply a thin layer of oil.** Pour about ½ teaspoon of oil onto a paper towel and rub it over the entire pan — inside, outside, handle, everything.

4. **Wipe it off.** This is the step everyone skips. Take a clean paper towel and wipe off as much oil as you can. The pan should look almost dry. Too much oil = sticky, uneven seasoning.

5. **Bake upside down.** Place the pan upside down in a 450°F (230°C) oven for 1 hour. Put foil on the rack below to catch any drips.

6. **Let it cool in the oven.** Turn off the oven and leave the pan inside until it's cool enough to handle.

7. **Repeat 2-3 times** for a new pan. After that, regular cooking maintains the seasoning.

That's it. Done.

## The Myths

### "Never use soap on cast iron"
**False.** Modern dish soap is mild detergent, not the lye-based soap your grandparents had. It won't strip seasoning. What WILL strip seasoning: prolonged soaking, the dishwasher, and acidic foods cooked for a long time.

### "You need flaxseed oil"
**Overrated.** Flaxseed oil creates a hard initial seasoning, but it can flake off over time. Regular vegetable or canola oil works great and is much cheaper.

### "You need to season it in the oven 6+ times before using it"
**Unnecessary.** Two or three rounds of oven seasoning, then just start cooking. The best seasoning comes from actual use — especially cooking with oil, frying, searing, and making cornbread.

### "Never cook acidic foods"
**Partially true.** A quick tomato sauce is fine. Simmering tomato soup for an hour in a newly seasoned pan will strip it. Once your seasoning is well-established (after a few months of regular use), even longer acid exposure is manageable.

## Daily Care (30 Seconds)

1. After cooking, rinse with hot water while the pan is still warm
2. If food is stuck, scrub with coarse salt and a paper towel
3. Dry on the stove over low heat for 1 minute
4. Rub with a tiny drop of oil on a paper towel

That's your daily routine. It takes less time than loading a dishwasher.

---

## When Things Go Wrong

**Sticky/tacky surface:** You used too much oil. Scrub it off and re-season with a thinner coat.

**Food sticking:** Your seasoning needs more time. Cook fattier foods (bacon, fried eggs with lots of butter) for a few sessions to build it up.

**Rust spots:** Scrub with steel wool, wash, dry thoroughly, and re-season. Rust happens when the pan stays wet. Always dry immediately.

**Flaking seasoning:** The original coat was too thick. Scrub to bare metal and start over with thinner coats.

---

## The Bottom Line

Cast iron is the most forgiving cookware you own. It's been used for hundreds of years by people who didn't overthink it. Season it simply, use it often, dry it after washing. Everything else is noise.
